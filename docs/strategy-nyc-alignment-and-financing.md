# STRATEGY-001: Aligning with the City, and Financing the Families

**Date:** 2026-08-23
**For:** Tiffany Lozado, Dean · IEP & Thrive / Cure Consulting Group
**Question 1:** How do we work *with* the Mamdani administration instead of against it, in NYC where our experience and demographic actually are?
**Question 2:** How do we close the financing gap when most of these families have no disposable income?
**Companions:** `docs/legal/nys-sped-school-regulatory-brief.md` · `docs/market-research-002-carter-day-school.md`

---

## The short answer

**These are the same question.**

The reason non-affluent families can't access a Carter school is that Carter requires someone to front $78,000 and wait. The reason the administration is hostile to Carter is that it costs $101,757 per student and produces nothing it can measure. **Both problems disappear the moment you get paid by the city directly instead of by winning a case against it.**

So the strategy is not "be a better Carter school." It is:

> **Build the thing the city would build if it could — then get paid the way the city wants to pay.**

Three mechanisms do that, in ascending order of time-to-revenue and descending order of difficulty. Run them in sequence, not in parallel.

## Part I — What the administration actually wants

Read the administration's own words and budget before designing anything.

**Mamdani, on special education:**
> *"It is time to actually deliver them the kind of education that would mean their families don't have to consider going to a private school system."*

He explicitly framed his approach as **not** "exclusively a legal approach" — distinguishing himself from predecessors who fought families in court. ([Gothamist, 2026](https://gothamist.com/news/mamdani-becomes-latest-mayor-hoping-to-fix-nycs-special-education-system))

**What the executive budget actually did:**

| Line | Amount | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Preschool special education expansion | **$67M** (250 seats, Nest/Horizon/AIMS/Path/ACES into Pre-K) | Building public capacity |
| Support for SWD in public schools | **$86M** | Building public capacity |
| **Contracted-out related services** (speech, OT) | **+$30M** | **Willing to pay private providers — for services** |
| Projected savings from avoided due-process litigation | **$147–149M** | **A number they have to hit** |
| Carter-case spending, next year | still **>$1.4B** | The problem is not solved |

Sources: [City Journal](https://www.city-journal.org/article/new-york-mamdani-budget-carter-cases-education-spending), [Gothamist](https://gothamist.com/news/mamdani-becomes-latest-mayor-hoping-to-fix-nycs-special-education-system)

**Three things follow directly from that table:**

1. **Contracting with private providers is politically acceptable to this administration.** They just added $30M of it. What is unacceptable is a $101,757 litigated tuition settlement.
2. **They have publicly booked $147–149M in litigation savings.** They now need mechanisms that produce those savings. A provider who walks in offering one is not an adversary — they are a solution to a problem the mayor has already promised to fix.
3. **They are building public capacity in the wrong places.** City Journal's sharpest criticism of the budget: the two new special-education schools opening fall 2026 target *"neither the populations filing for private placement nor the neighborhoods generating the most due-process cases."* **That is a published, named, unfilled gap.**

**What the Comptroller has already asked for.** The "Course Correction" report (Lander, Aug 2023) laid out the policy blueprint that is still the reference document:

- A **$100M "Pay for Success"** network of qualified, multilingual providers **contracted by DOE**
- **Raise DOE contract rates** — currently $45 per 30-minute speech session against a $300/hr private market
- **Create provider standards** — because *"for over 90% of the more than 900 special education service providers paid by DOE in FY22"* there was *"no safety clearance, accreditation check, or responsibility determination prior to payment"*

Read that last finding twice. **The city is paying nine hundred providers it has not vetted.** A provider that voluntarily arrives credentialed, background-checked, accredited, and reporting outcomes is supplying something the Comptroller formally requested and essentially nobody offers.

**What advocates say is missing** (Advocates for Children, Bronx Legal Services): 7,500 students went without speech therapy last year; 5,400 finished the year without mandated counseling; 13,800 related-service recommendations went unfulfilled K–12; nearly 10,000 preschoolers missed mandated services entirely. And the shortfalls fall hardest in districts with more Black and Hispanic students.

## Part II — The three mechanisms

### Mechanism 1 — Contract as a DOE service provider *(revenue in months, no approval needed)*

This is Door F from the regulatory brief, and it is available **now**.

**Why it works politically:** the budget just funded it. The Comptroller recommended expanding exactly this network and raising its rates. It creates no Carter exposure, no litigation, no receivable risk.

**Why it works commercially:** DOE pays on contract, not on judgment. Cash cycle measured in weeks, not a year. And the FCNY Returnable Grant Fund exists specifically to bridge registered City contracts, interest-free.

**Why it works for us specifically:** Tiffany's network — CSE chairs, district and DOE special-education administrators — is warmest here. This is the one mechanism where the founder's existing relationships are the go-to-market.

**What to sell:** IEP-aligned instructional intervention and progress monitoring in the districts with the highest unfulfilled-service rates. Arrive pre-vetted — background clearances, credentialing documentation, accreditation pathway, and a published outcome-reporting commitment — and you have answered Comptroller recommendation #6 before being asked.

**Strategic function:** revenue now, and the DOE relationship that makes Mechanism 3 possible. This is the on-ramp, not the destination.

---

### Mechanism 2 — A direct-payment school, built as the bridge *(12–24 months)*

Door D, but structured for the families who currently can't get in — and designed from day one to be evidence for Mechanism 3.

Two design choices make this politically defensible rather than politically exposed:

**Price visibly below the settlement average.** At $76,500 against a $101,757 average settlement — before DOE's own legal costs on the other side — every student we serve is a documented saving. Publish that number.

**Publish outcomes nobody else publishes.** Per the Manhattan Institute, these schools *"are not required to report student progress, graduation rates, attendance, or postsecondary outcomes,"* and consequently *"NYC DOE cannot evaluate program quality or effectiveness."* Every incumbent shares this vulnerability. Being the first to publish costs almost nothing and converts us from part of the problem into the only data point the city has.

**Then make the offer nobody has made:** *price below litigation, and put a portion of tuition at risk against documented IEP goal attainment.* The Comptroller asked for "Pay for Success." No school in this market has ever offered it. It is the single most credible way to walk into DOE as a partner rather than a claimant.

---

### Mechanism 3 — NYSED approval, argued from the city's own budget *(18–36 months, the endgame)*

This is the move that changes the game, and it rests on an argument nobody has made to SEQA.

**The regional-need test asks whether districts are failing to place students of a given profile.** In MARKET-002 I read that as a barrier, because districts can usually serve mild-to-moderate learning differences in-district. But look at what NYC's own budget says:

> **NYC is paying $723M to place roughly 7,600 students privately, through litigation, because it could not place them itself.**

That is not market demand. That is a district documenting, in its own budget, at scale, and in public, that it cannot place these students. **The Carter data is the regional-need evidence.** As far as I can find, no applicant has ever framed it that way.

**And DOE's incentive now runs with us, not against us:**

| | Carter placement | Approved-school placement |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to city | ~$101,757 average settlement | NYSED-set rate **[VERIFY actual NYC 853 day rates with the Rate Setting Unit — do not model without this]** |
| Legal cost | Both sides, plus fee-shifting to the district | None |
| Time to placement | ~152 days average resolution | Immediate |
| Budget predictability | Overran $935M budget to $1.3B | Rate-set and budgetable |
| Counts toward the $147–149M savings pledge | No | **Yes** |

**Approval also unlocks the Nickerson letter (P-1).** When DOE fails to place a student within 60 school days, it issues a voucher good at a **state-approved** nonpublic school. That is DOE routing families to us, voluntarily, with zero litigation and zero receivable. It is the cleanest revenue in this entire analysis and it is available only to approved schools.

**And it fills the gap City Journal named.** Target the profiles that are actually filing for private placement, in the neighborhoods actually generating the due-process cases. That is a one-sentence pitch to a Deputy Chancellor.

> ### ⚠️ The trade, stated honestly
> Approval means NYSED sets your tuition rate, caps non-direct costs at 35%, requires a Regents charter (nonprofit), imposes a 16-student minimum, and **bars any parent payment on top of public payment**. Lower margin, less pricing control, more compliance.
>
> In exchange: no litigation, no receivable, no working-capital requirement, no political risk, and DOE as a willing buyer. **And it solves the family financing problem completely, because the city pays directly and the family pays nothing.**
>
> **[VERIFY with counsel]** whether an approved school may simultaneously enroll unilaterally-placed Carter families. The assurance language covers *"students placed according to NYS procedures,"* which arguably leaves unilateral placements outside it — but this is genuinely unsettled and must not be assumed. Sequence the mechanisms rather than running Doors A and D simultaneously without advice.

## Part III — Closing the financing gap

### Layer 0 — Two legal mechanics to get right before anything else

**Attorney fees shift to the district.** Under IDEA, prevailing parents recover attorney fees from the school district. Families generally do **not** pay lawyers out of pocket, and many NY special-education firms work on that basis. The barrier for a working family is tuition, not representation — and most families don't know this. Saying it plainly is itself an access intervention.

**Never discount tuition. Defer it.** This is the technical point that decides whether the model works. **The district reimburses what the family was legally obligated to pay.** A $20,000 discount reduces the recoverable amount by $20,000 — you give away the money twice. The correct structure is **full tuition, with a deferred and contingent payment obligation**: the family signs for the full amount, payment is deferred pending the funding outcome, and the school looks to the district. **[VERIFY with NY special-education counsel]** — this is standard practice, but the enrollment contract has to be drafted precisely or the obligation fails and the recovery fails with it.

### Layer 1 — Direct payment, not reimbursement

Reimbursement presumes the family fronted the money — which is precisely the exclusion. **Direct payment (*Connors*-type relief) orders the district to pay the school.** This single structural choice is the difference between Vincent Smith, which states plainly that it *"does not offer financial aid or scholarships,"* and a school that a Bronx or Queens family can actually attend.

### Layer 2 — Tier the seats by risk, and never open one you haven't financed

| Tier | Who | Risk | Funding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Pendency continuers — already prevailed, payment already flowing | Near zero | Self-funding |
| **B** | Represented new cases, strong evidentiary record | Moderate | Prepay pool + facility |
| **C** | Unrepresented families, longest cycle | High | **Guarantee pool only** |

Cap Tier C at exactly what the guarantee covers. This is the discipline that keeps the mission from killing the school.

### Layer 3 — Instruments, most capital-efficient first

**1. Philanthropic first-loss guarantee — the highest-leverage ask by far.**
A foundation commits, say, $500K that is drawn *only if cases lose*. At an 85%+ win rate, a $500K guarantee can support $3–4M of receivables while rarely being touched. Nothing else in this list is close on leverage.

The pitch writes itself: **white students are 71% of NYC tuition-payment recipients against 12.5% of the public-school disability population, while Black and Latino students are ~75% of that population and 24% of recipients.** A guarantee pool that exists specifically to seat the excluded 75% is one of the cleanest equity grants in New York education philanthropy.

**2. Program-related investments (PRIs)** from foundations, typically 1–3%.

**3. [Fund for the City of New York — Operating Loan Fund](https://www.fcny.org/nonprofit-loan-financing/).** Interest-free, one-time 2% service fee, 60–90 day terms, **48-hour response and 3–5 business day approval**, open to any 501(c)(3) serving the NYC metro area awaiting committed government or foundation funds. Right instrument for *settled cases awaiting payment*. Note the 60–90 day term is shorter than a full case cycle — this bridges the tail, not the whole thing.

**4. [FCNY Returnable Grant Fund](https://www.fcny.org/nonprofit-loan-financing/).** Interest-free and fee-free, for organizations with **registered City of New York contracts** experiencing registration or release delays. This is the instrument for Mechanism 1 revenue.

**5. [Nonprofit Finance Fund](https://nff.org/our-work/financing/).** Federally certified CDFI, $1.7B deployed since 1980, explicitly *"helps nonprofits access capital while waiting for revenue from government contracts"* and bridges receivables. Right instrument for a revolving facility once you have 18–24 months of settled-case history to underwrite against.

**6. Prepay discount mix.** Vincent Smith's 10%-off-by-September-1 is a working-capital instrument wearing a discount's clothing. Families who can prepay effectively finance the deferred seats. Run it deliberately.

### Layer 4 — The structural decision both answers converge on

**Every instrument above except the last requires 501(c)(3) status.** A for-profit LLC can access essentially none of it — no PRIs, no FCNY, no CDFI terms, no foundation guarantee.

And Mechanism 3 requires a **Regents charter as a not-for-profit** under 8 NYCRR §200.7(a)(2)(i)(d) regardless.

> **So the single most consequential decision is corporate structure, and both questions point the same way: a 501(c)(3) with a Regents charter, related to but distinct from Cure Consulting Group.** Make this decision first. Everything else is downstream of it. **[VERIFY with counsel]** how a Cure-affiliated nonprofit can be structured without breaching the RCM's related-party and 35% non-direct-cost rules under a future approval.

### Layer 5 — Shorten the cycle

- **Pendency attaches immediately** for continuing students — payment starts at filing, not at resolution
- **Resolution sessions occur within 30 days** of a complaint; many cases can settle there
- Settle rather than litigate wherever the record is strong
- Negotiate **interim and partial payment agreements** rather than waiting for a single end-of-case payment
- OATH has already cut average resolution from 282 days to **152 days (2025)** — the cycle is improving without us

## Part IV — The sequence

| Phase | Window | Move | Revenue | Financing need |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | Now | Keep running the summer intensive. It is the pipeline, the outcome dataset, and the proof of practice | Existing | None |
| **1** | 0–12 mo | **Form the 501(c)(3).** Contract with DOE as a vetted related-services and instructional provider in the highest-unfulfilled-service districts | Contract revenue in months | FCNY Returnable Grant Fund |
| **2** | 6–24 mo | Assemble the guarantee pool. Open the school small — **Tier A pendency-heavy** — on a direct-payment structure, priced below the settlement average, **publishing outcomes from day one** | $0.7–0.9M yr 1 | Guarantee pool + FCNY + prepay mix |
| **3** | 18–36 mo | File for NYSED approval. Regional need argued from **the city's own Carter budget** plus two years of our own outcome data. Target the profiles and neighborhoods City Journal says the city is missing | Rate-set, no receivable | **None — DOE pays directly** |
| **4** | 36 mo+ | Approved status → **P-1 / Nickerson eligibility** → DOE routes families to us with no litigation. The financing problem dissolves | Predictable | None |

**The Carter phase is a bridge, not the destination.** It buys the track record, the outcome data, and the credibility that make approval achievable — and approval is what makes the school accessible to families with no disposable income, permanently.

We publish outcomes no school in this market publishes, and we'll put a portion of tuition at risk against documented IEP goal attainment. We're asking for regional-need certification, not a settlement.
