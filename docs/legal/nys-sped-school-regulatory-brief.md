# Operating a SPED Academy on Public Money in New York State

**Regulatory brief — research memo, not legal advice.** Prepared 2026-08-23 for IEP & Thrive / Cure Consulting Group.
Every substantive claim below is sourced to statute, Commissioner's Regulations, or a current NYSED document. Items marked **[VERIFY]** need confirmation from NY education counsel or the named NYSED unit before you rely on them.

---

## 0. Bottom line up front

"Receiving DOE or public money" is not one thing in New York. There are seven distinct legal doors, and they have almost nothing in common. Picking the wrong one wastes a year.

The door most people mean — a private special-education school that districts place students into and pay for — is **NYSED approval as a private school-age (5–21) special education program under Article 89 of the Education Law**, governed by 8 NYCRR §200.7. Colloquially, an **"853 school"** (Chapter 853 of the Laws of 1976).

**Three structural facts about that door conflict directly with a "start with one cohort of 6, charge $3,500–$4,000" plan:**

1. **You cannot start with 6 students.** 8 NYCRR §200.7(c)(3) sets a **16-student minimum** for programs approved for reimbursement after September 1, 1981.
2. **You do not set your own tuition.** NYSED's Rate Setting Unit sets your per-diem rate from your reported costs under the Reimbursable Cost Manual. Your price is an output of an audit formula, not a market decision.
3. **You cannot charge parents anything on top.** The application's binding Certification and Assurances states: *"Parents of students will not be asked to make any payments in lieu of, in advance of or in addition to, State, school district or county payments for allowable costs for students placed according to NYS procedures."* A publicly placed seat and a $875 private deposit cannot be the same seat.

And there is a gate before all three: **regional need**. NYSED will not accept an application without it, and need is proven by *school districts naming actual students they are actively failing to place* — not by market demand, parent interest, or the strength of your program.

> **Strategic read:** The 853 door is built for students whose needs a district and its BOCES genuinely cannot meet. A small-group Orton-Gillingham literacy and executive-function academy for students with IEPs and mild-to-moderate learning differences is, in the eyes of a CSE, usually serviceable in-district. That is the central obstacle — not paperwork, not capital.
>
> The door that actually fits that profile is **Door D: unilateral parental placement with tuition reimbursement (Carter/Burlington)**, which requires *no state approval at all*. It is how a large share of New York's small private SPED schools are in fact funded.

## 2. Door A — Approved private school-age SPED program (the "853" route)

### 2.1 Gate 1: Regional need — the real filter

**You cannot even submit an application without it.** From the NYSED initial application instructions:

> *"Prior to submitting an application, the applicant must provide the NYSED Office of Special Education, Special Education Quality Assurance (SEQA) Office with documentation that there is a demonstrated need... A Determination of Regional Need form must accompany your application. **Applications will not be accepted without a determination of regional need.**"*

The process:

1. You file a **Written Request for Determination of Regional Need** with the SEQA regional office where the school will sit. For Nassau/Suffolk that is the **Long Island Regional Unit, (631) 952-3352**. For NYC, **(718) 722-4544**.
2. You describe the proposed classes, ratios, ages, disability categories, learner profile, and the BOCES regions (or NYC geographic districts) you would serve.
3. SEQA sends a **District Needs Identification Survey** to school districts in the region. Districts return it **directly to NYSED — not to you** (student confidentiality).
4. Each district checks one of two boxes:
   - *"The school district is not seeking placement for any students similar in profile to the students the new program is proposing to serve"*, **or**
   - a roster of named students the CSE is *actively seeking placement for*, which may only include students with documentation of: efforts to place in less restrictive public settings; efforts to support them in less restrictive settings with supplementary aids and services; and lack of progress in prior/less restrictive placements. **A Regional Associate verifies this.**
5. Supplemental letters from parents or BOCES may be attached, but **"Determination of regional need cannot be based solely on supplemental information."**

**Why this matters for your model:** the survey is explicitly a *restrictiveness* test, not a *quality* test. A district that can serve a 3rd grader with dyslexia in a resource room or 12:1:1 will check box one. Regional need is demonstrable for profiles districts genuinely cannot place — significant emotional/behavioral needs, severe multiple disabilities, intensive management needs — which is a different program, a different staffing model, and a different insurance and facilities posture than a summer literacy intensive.

### 2.2 Gate 2: Corporate form

Per §200.7(a)(2)(i)(d):

- **In-state not-for-profit:** a **charter or application for a charter from the Board of Regents**, incorporating a school authorized to provide special education services. Regents charters issue under **Educ. Law §216** via the NYSED Office of Counsel; new schools normally get a **provisional charter (typically 3 years, $100 fee)**, then extensions, then an absolute charter. Petition goes to Office of Counsel, is routed to program offices, then onto a Board of Regents agenda.
- **In-state for-profit:** **approval by the Commissioner of the school's incorporation for the provision of special education.** For-profit is contemplated (the application's assurances reference "private for-profit and not-for-profit agencies"), but it is the less-traveled path and the Regents route is the norm in this sector.

Cure Consulting Group's existing for-profit structure does **not** carry over automatically. **[VERIFY]** with counsel how a Cure-affiliated entity would be structured — a separate 501(c)(3) with a Regents charter is the conventional answer, and it has real consequences for control, related-party transactions (see RCM limits), and any exit.

### 2.3 Gate 3: Capital

§200.7(a)(1)(iv):

> *"Private schools seeking initial approval to be reimbursed with public funds shall have access to sufficient capital or other financial resources, **other than revenues expected from New York State or local school districts**, to cover all operating, property maintenance, leasing or purchase costs during the year of conditional approval."*

You must fund a full year of operations without a dollar of the public money you are applying to receive. The evaluation criteria restate this as: *"The operational budget is reasonable and demonstrates the program has sufficient resources, funding options and financial means to operate for the first year."*

### 2.4 Gate 4: Physical plant

- **Fire safety check by the NYS Division of Fire Prevention and Control** (§200.7(a)(2)(i)(c)) — in-state schools.
- Annual fire inspection thereafter per Educ. Law §807-a (§200.7(b)(7)).
- Line-drawing **floor plans for every site**, including administrative sites.
- Accessibility documentation for exterior and interior routes at each site.
- Building lease or amortization schedule per site; proof of current liability insurance. Lease/mortgage costs must be *"reasonable and affordable for the region."*

"A dedicated community space on Long Island" as currently described on the marketing site will not clear this. You need a leased, inspected, accessible, plan-documented facility before approval.

### 2.5 The application itself

Submitted as Word or PDF to **OSEapplications@nysed.gov**. Eight parts:

| Part | Contents |
|------|----------|
| Certification & Assurances | 16 binding assurances, CEO signature |
| I — General Information | Applicant info, charter/incorporation attachments, program types (Day/Residential), every site |
| II — Program Description | Program model + sample daily schedule; 17–18 narrative policy items; behavior management policy; psychotropic medication policy; school/summer calendar; org chart; staffing plan with certifications; Student/Staff Data table by class |
| III — Physical Plant | Health & safety attachments per site, floor plans, accessibility |
| IV — Fiscal | 10 narrative items, leases, liability insurance, Schedules 1–3 budget (direct vs. non-direct care per RCM), internal controls, whistleblower policy, cost allocation methodology |
| V — Character & Competence | Notarized 16-item review of each chief executive, plus resume and licenses |
| VI — Governance | 9 narrative items; founding board must read the RCM "Statement on the Governance Role of a Trustee or Board Member" |
| VII — Payee Info / substitute W-9 | Mailed with original signature |

**Review timeline:** NYSED preliminary-reviews for completeness; you get 30 days to cure omissions; **review generally completed within 60 days of a *complete* application**; may include an onsite visit. *"Applications that do not include all documentation at the time of submission will be considered incomplete and will not be processed."*

**Approval is two-stage:**
- **Conditional approval** — maximum one school year. **You may not operate before written conditional approval issues.**
- **Final approval** — requires **at least two NYSED site visits during the conditional year**, plus documented completion of NYSED training on **Consolidated Fiscal Reporting (CFR)** and **governance**.

Denial gets a written explanation; you may submit a revised application within 30 days.

### 2.6 Ongoing operating standards (§200.7(b), (c), (e))

- **180 days of instruction per year** (§200.7(b)(5)).
- Full-day / half-day session length per §200.1(q) / §200.1(v) (§200.7(b)(4)); the day must include **both instructional and related services**.
- **All professional instructional and supervisory personnel appropriately certified** under Part 80 (§200.7(b)(6)).
- **No corporal punishment, aversive interventions, or seclusion** as defined in 8 NYCRR §19.5 (§200.7(b)(8)).
- Discipline policy aligned to §100.2(l)(1)(i); student discipline per Part 201 (§200.7(b)(3)).
- Records confidentiality comparable to districts, per §200.2(b)(6) (§200.7(b)(2)).
- Child abuse reporting: Educ. Law Art. 23-B and Soc. Serv. Law §§413–420 (§200.7(b)(9)).
- **Annual application for continued placement of each student, due June 1** (§200.7(c)(1)).
- **Annual educational progress report** per student against IEP annual goals, to the referring CSE (§200.7(c)(4)).
- No student may be removed or transferred without the contracting district's approval.
- You may not exceed the total capacity on your NYSED approval chart.
- **90 days' written notice before closing**, with a student transfer plan (§200.7(e)).

**Class sizes** are governed by 8 NYCRR §200.6(h) — the familiar school-age special class ratios, including **6:1+1** (highly intensive management needs), **8:1+1** (intensive management needs), and **12:1+4** (severe multiple disabilities, primarily habilitation and treatment), alongside the standard 12:1+1 and 15:1 options. Your approval chart fixes which ratios you may operate and how many of each. **[VERIFY]** the full current ratio table in §200.6(h) against the exact profile you intend to serve.

**Termination exposure** (§200.7(a)(3)): the Commissioner notifies in writing of deficiencies; you have 30 days to reply or termination takes effect; you may request a hearing within 10 business days. **Removal in 5 business days** where there is a *"clear and present danger to health or safety,"* expressly including evidence of corporal punishment or seclusion.

### 2.7 The economics: you do not price your own product

Tuition rates are set by the **NYSED Rate Setting Unit (RSU)** under **8 NYCRR §200.9** and the **Reimbursable Cost Manual (RCM)**. From the RSU's 2025-26 school-age methodology memo (July 1, 2025):

- Rates for 2025-26 are **based on reported 2023-24 financial data** — a **two-year lag** between spending and reimbursement.
- A **2.9% prospective trend factor** for 2025-26 (7.32% compounded with 2024-25's 4.3%), applied to reimbursable base-year direct and non-direct care costs.
- A **non-direct care cost parameter caps administrative/overhead reimbursement at 35% of reimbursable costs** after adjustments (computed as direct care × 0.5385).
- **Total cost screens** cap year-over-year per-diem growth (generally the prior reconciliation per-diem plus 2.9%), with adjustments tied to enrollment-to-capacity ratios.
- **Reconciliation** under §200.9(f)(3)(iii): interim → prospective → reconciliation → final rate, with recovery of overpayments. Rates are **subject to audit** after the fact.
- Applied revenues are netted against costs.

Three consequences worth internalizing:

1. **Cash flow is brutal for a new program.** The published methodology addresses programs in operation during the base year; it does not describe how a brand-new program with no base-year CFR gets its first rate. **[VERIFY] directly with the Rate Setting Unit (518-474-3227, rateweb@nysed.gov) how a first-year program's interim rate is established** — this single answer determines whether the model is financeable.
2. **Overhead is capped at 35%.** Marketing, management fees, and any Cure Consulting Group services charged to the school are squeezed into that cap and are subject to RCM related-party and executive-compensation rules.
3. **Surplus is not yours to keep freely.** Reconciliation and audit claw back rate dollars not supported by allowable costs.

### 2.8 The 16-student minimum

§200.7(c)(3): *"Educational programs initially approved for reimbursement after September 1, 1981 shall provide instruction to a minimum of 16 students by September 1, 1982."*

The drafting is vestigial — the 1982 date has long passed — but the operative rule NYSED applies is a **16-student floor for programs approved after September 1, 1981**. **[VERIFY]** with the SEQA Nondistrict Unit (518-473-1185) how the 16-student minimum is applied to a new approval today, and whether it is measured at approval, at conditional-to-final conversion, or on a rolling basis. This is the provision that most directly contradicts a one-cohort-of-6 launch.

### 2.9 One benefit worth noting

**8 NYCRR §130.3(a)(2)** deems a *"State-approved private special education school"* **substantially equivalent** for compulsory-education purposes without a local school authority review. Approval therefore also solves the Part 130 substantial-equivalency problem that any *unapproved* private school in New York now faces (see §5.4).

## 3. Door B — Approved preschool SPED program, ages 3–5 (§4410)

A materially more open door than Door A, and worth serious consideration.

- **Legal basis:** Educ. Law §4410; 8 NYCRR §200.16 (services), §200.19 (reimbursement), §200.20 (approval, operation, administration).
- **Approval:** §200.20(a)(1) grants **conditional approval** on meeting §200.7(a)(2)(i)(a)–(d) — i.e., largely the same evidentiary gates as Door A — and operations must be consistent with §200.7(a)(1)–(3) and (b)(3), (5), (6), (8), (9), (10). Also **180 days minimum** (§200.20(a)(6)).
- **Program types you can be approved for:** Multidisciplinary Evaluation (MDE), **Special Education Itinerant Services (SEIS/SEIT)**, **Special Class in an Integrated Setting (SCIS)**, and **Special Class (SC)**.
- **Leadership qualifications:** Executive Director needs a bachelor's in business or education, or NYS certification in special education (§200.20(b)(3)(i)); Educational Director needs a NYS teaching certificate valid for students with disabilities, **birth–grade 2** (§200.20(b)(5)).
- **Applications:** initial and modification packets at nysed.gov/special-education/preschool-applications, submitted to **OSEapplications@nysed.gov**.

**Why this door is more open right now:**

- New York is in an acknowledged **capacity and staffing crisis** in preschool special education. An OSC performance audit found SED unable to ensure all preschool students with disabilities have access to required services; sector data shows roughly **357 certified SPED teachers left 4410/853 programs in a single year (~17% loss)** and **78% of 4410 providers reported at least one teacher vacancy**.
- NYC published a **regional need map for Spring 2026** (data as of Oct 31, 2025) and states that **NYSED will consider Special Class applications for programs in or adjacent to any district with "some," "more," or "most" need — and will accept SCIS applications for *all* districts.** That is a far lower bar than the school-age district-survey process.
- NYC announced (July 2026) a **$67.5M plan adding ~250 preschool special education seats**.
- NYSED is studying **alternative tuition rate-setting methodologies for 4410 providers** — the current rate structure is under active reform pressure.

**SEIT/SEIS in particular** is worth modeling: it is itinerant, ratio-light, and does not require the facility build-out that a Special Class does. **[VERIFY]** the current county/state cost-sharing split for §4410 placements and the SEIT rate structure with the RSU, and whether Nassau/Suffolk counties have published need or procurement postures.

**The trade-off:** ages 3–5 is not the market the current IEP & Thrive brand, curriculum, or copy targets (K–6th, CSE-ready reports, September readiness). This is a genuinely different business.

## 4. Door C — Extended School Year / July–August (§4408)

**This is the closest legal analogue to the program you already run — and the current program does not qualify.**

- **Eligibility:** the CSE must determine the student needs 12-month service to prevent **substantial regression** — "inability to maintain developmental levels due to a loss of skill or knowledge," with a review/reteaching period of **eight weeks or more** at the start of the school year as the marker.
- **Who may operate ESY:** **only NYSED-approved entities** — districts, BOCES, nonpublic schools with approved special education programs, state-supported/state-operated schools, and approved preschool programs. *"New providers must apply to NYSED for approval before operating."* There is no separate, lighter "summer-only" approval; ESY rides on Door A or Door B approval.
- **Program structure required:** **minimum 30 days during July and August, five days per week.** Full-day instruction: **≥5 hours (K–6)** or **≥5.5 hours (grades 7–12)**. Half-day: ≥2.5 hours (K–6) or ≥3 hours (7–12).
- **Funding:** the student's **county of residence contributes 10%** of the approved cost of July/August services for ages 5–21 under Educ. Law §4408; districts are reimbursed **80% of approved total costs** for education (and maintenance/transportation where applicable) via **STAC** filings. Funding approval must be granted through the STAC/Medicaid Unit per student. STAC records will not process if services are inconsistent with the IEP or with the conditions of your NYSED approval.
- **Explicit exclusion:** *"School districts are not required to pay for the enrollment and other fees at summer recreational or **nonapproved educational programs**"* parents independently select.

**Measured against the current product:** 4 hrs/day × Mon–Thu × 6 weeks = **24 days at 4 hours**. That fails the 30-day requirement, fails the 5-day-per-week requirement, and fails the 5-hour full-day minimum. Restructuring to **Mon–Fri, 5+ hours, 30 days** is a prerequisite to ESY viability — and is only meaningful *after* Door A or B approval.

## 5. Door D — Unilateral parental placement and tuition reimbursement (no approval required)

This is the path that actually fits a small, high-quality, mild-to-moderate private SPED academy, and it is how a meaningful share of New York's private SPED schools are funded — especially in NYC.

### 5.1 How it works

Under IDEA and *Sch. Comm. of Burlington v. Dep't of Educ.* / *Florence County Sch. Dist. Four v. Carter*, a parent who believes the district's IEP denies FAPE may unilaterally enroll the child in a private school and seek reimbursement through an **impartial hearing (IHO)**, appealable to a **State Review Officer (SRO)** and then to federal or state court.

The three-prong test:
1. The district **failed to offer FAPE**;
2. The **parent's unilateral placement is appropriate**; and
3. **Equitable considerations** support the award.

**Critically: state approval is irrelevant to prong 2.** *A parent's failure to select a state-approved program in favor of an unapproved one is not itself a bar to reimbursement.* Your school does not need to be on any NYSED list.

### 5.2 Burden and equities

In New York, districts generally bear the burden of proof in IDEA due process hearings, **except** that a parent seeking tuition reimbursement for a unilateral placement bears the burden of persuasion and production **on the appropriateness of the placement**. Awards can be reduced or denied where the parent failed to give the statutorily required notice, refused to cooperate, withheld private evaluations, or was not genuinely open to a public program.

### 5.3 Reimbursement vs. direct payment ("Connors")

Reimbursement presumes the family fronted the tuition. Where a family cannot afford to, New York practice recognizes **direct-payment (often called *Connors*) relief**, ordering the district to pay the school directly. This distinction is the difference between a school serving only affluent families and one with broader reach — and it materially changes your collections model and your enrollment contract. **[VERIFY]** current SRO/SDNY/EDNY posture on direct payment with special education counsel; this area moves.

### 5.4 What you still must comply with

Being unapproved does **not** mean unregulated:

- You are a **nonpublic school** subject to **Part 130 substantial equivalency**. Under 8 NYCRR §130.3, you are deemed substantially equivalent only if you fit an enumerated pathway — registration, **NYSED-approved special education school status (§130.3(a)(2))**, approved accreditation, IB, DoD approval, or approved assessments. Otherwise the **Local School Authority conducts a substantial equivalency review** (reviews for schools operating as of 9/28/2022 ran through the 2024-25 year and repeat every seven years). A new school should plan its pathway deliberately — **accreditation under §130.3(a)(3) is the usual answer for an unapproved private SPED school.**
- **BEDS code** from the State Office of Religious and Independent Schools — one per school building.
- Standard school obligations: fingerprinting/background clearance, mandated reporter training, DASA, health services, immunization, fire safety, building code, ADA.
- Employment, insurance, and FERPA/records obligations.

### 5.5 Honest assessment of this door

**Pros:** no NYSED approval, no regional need survey, no 16-student floor, no rate-setting cap, no 35% overhead parameter, no ban on private tuition, no two-year cash-flow lag. You set your price. You can start with six students.

**Cons:** revenue is litigated family-by-family and is never guaranteed; families need counsel; timelines are long; you carry accounts-receivable risk on cases that settle late or lose; the model is criticized publicly (see the Manhattan Institute's critique of NYC "Carter case" spending) and is a recurring target for legislative and budgetary reform. Your revenue depends on a remedy the State would prefer to shrink.

## 6. Door E — Nickerson / P-1 letter (NYC only)

If NYC DOE fails to offer a placement within the required timeline (commonly framed as **60 school days**), the family may obtain a **Nickerson letter (P-1)** — a voucher letting the child attend a **state-approved non-public school** for one year at DOE expense.

**The catch: it only works for state-approved schools.** Door E is downstream of Door A. It is a reason to pursue approval, not an alternative to it. Also: one year only (occasionally renewed), the IEP must match the services the school offers, and the family does the school search.

## 7. Door F — Contracted vendor to districts and BOCES

The lowest-friction way to touch public money without becoming a school:

- Districts may **contract for special education services** under Educ. Law §4402(2). BOCES purchase services cooperatively across Nassau/Suffolk.
- Related services (speech, OT, PT, counseling) delivered by appropriately licensed/certified personnel under contract.
- Professional development, IEP-goal-alignment consulting, and progress-monitoring services sold to districts as vendors.
- Note §200.7(b)(4)'s requirement that an approved school's day include instruction *and* related services — related-services contracting into approved schools is itself a market (NYSED has issued guidance specifically on the provision of related services to students placed in approved private schools in NYC).

This door requires vendor registration, insurance, and procurement compliance — not program approval. It generates revenue and, more importantly, **generates the district relationships that a future regional-need determination depends on.**

---

## 8. Door G — Charter school

Educ. Law Article 56. Public funding per pupil, no tuition. **A charter school cannot restrict admission to students with disabilities** — enrollment is by open lottery, and charters must serve all students. A "specialized focused school for SPED students" is structurally incompatible with the charter model. Not a viable route to the stated goal.

## 9. What this means for the current plan

### Direct conflicts with the marketing site as written

| Current plan element | Conflict |
|---|---|
| "Cohorts limited to 6 students" | §200.7(c)(3) 16-student minimum for approved programs |
| $3,500 / $4,000 tuition, self-set | RSU sets the rate under §200.9 + RCM for publicly funded seats |
| $875 / $1,000 non-refundable deposit | Assurance barring **any** parent payment in addition to public payment, for publicly placed students |
| "25% non-refundable deposit holds your spot" | Approved programs cannot condition a publicly placed seat on parent payment |
| 4 hrs/day, Mon–Thu, 6 weeks | ESY under §4408 requires 30 days, 5 days/week, ≥5 hrs (K–6) |
| "Dedicated community space, exact location provided upon enrollment" | Approval requires named, leased, fire-inspected, accessible sites with floor plans, pre-approval |
| Mild-to-moderate learning-differences positioning | Regional need survey tests whether districts *cannot* place these students |
| For-profit Cure Consulting Group program | §200.7(a)(2)(i)(d) requires a Regents charter (NFP) or Commissioner-approved incorporation (FP); 35% non-direct cost cap constrains management fees |

**None of this affects the current private-pay summer program**, which is lawful as a private educational service. The conflicts arise only when you attach public funding to the same seats.

### Recommended sequencing

**Phase 0 — now, no regulatory change required.** Keep running the private-pay summer intensive. It is your proof of outcomes, your parent base, and your credibility with CSEs. Nothing in this memo asks you to stop.

**Phase 1 — build the record (12–18 months).**
- Call the **SEQA Long Island Regional Unit, (631) 952-3352**, and ask directly: *what student profiles does Nassau/Suffolk currently lack placements for?* This is a free, fast answer to the question that determines everything.
- Call the **Rate Setting Unit, (518) 474-3227**, and ask how a first-year program's interim rate is set. **[VERIFY]**
- Call the **SEQA Nondistrict Unit, (518) 473-1185**, on how the 16-student minimum applies today. **[VERIFY]**
- Pursue **Door F** — contract with districts or BOCES for related services or IEP consulting. Revenue now, relationships that later produce regional-need survey responses.
- Retain **NY special education counsel** (not general corporate counsel).

**Phase 2 — pick a door deliberately.**
- If the honest answer from SEQA is that districts can place your target profile: build **Door D**. Incorporate as a school, secure a facility, get a BEDS code, choose a **§130.3(a)(3) accreditation pathway**, and build referral relationships with the NYC/Long Island parent-side special education bar. Expect and plan for reimbursement-litigation-driven revenue.
- If SEQA identifies a genuine unmet profile you can credibly serve: pursue **Door A**, budgeting **18–30 months** and **a full year of operating capital from non-public sources**, and restructure the entity accordingly.
- If you are willing to move down-age: **Door B** is currently the widest-open door in the state, with published need, active expansion funding, and rate reform in motion. **SEIT/SEIS is the lowest-capital entry point in this entire memo.**

**Phase 3 — ESY (Door C) only after A or B, with the schedule rebuilt to 30 days × 5 days × 5+ hours.**
