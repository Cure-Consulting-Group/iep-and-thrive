# MARKET-002: Carter-Funded Private SPED Day School (NY Metro) — Research Report

**Date:** 2026-08-23
**Prepared for:** IEP & Thrive (Cure Consulting Group) · Founder: Tiffany Lozado, Dean
**Decision:** Whether to build a year-round private special-education day school funded through IDEA unilateral-placement reimbursement ("Door D" / Carter–Burlington), as the long-term vehicle beyond the summer intensive
**Supersedes:** Nothing. MARKET-001 covers the private-pay summer intensive and remains valid.
**Companion:** `docs/legal/nys-sped-school-regulatory-brief.md` (regulatory analysis of all seven funding routes)
**Confidence:** Medium-High on market size and economics · Medium on political durability · **Low on Long Island geography**
**Recommendation:** **Enter with conditions** — the model works, but not at the address currently assumed

---

## 1. Executive Summary

New York City pays roughly **$723M/year in private-school tuition for students with disabilities to about 7,600 students** — an average near **$100,000 per student** — plus another ~$400M in related services, and that tuition line has grown **over 300% in a decade** (Source: [Chalkbeat, 2026-05-06](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/05/06/nyc-special-education-carter-case-tuition-racial-disparities/)). This is a real, large, cash-paying market that requires **no NYSED approval** to enter, and once a family prevails, **pendency ("stay-put") guarantees uninterrupted payment year over year** — the best retention mechanic in education services.

Three findings should reshape the plan:

1. **This is a New York City market, not a Long Island market.** NYC accounted for **92–98% of all due-process filings statewide**, while filings in the entire rest of the state *fell* from 438 to 358 over the same period (Source: [Manhattan Institute / ERIC, 2026](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED679657.pdf)). Carter density is **14.8 cases per 1,000 students on the Upper West Side and 6.7 in parts of Brooklyn, but under 1.0 in Queens and 0.6 on Staten Island.** A Nassau campus sits on the wrong side of that gradient.
2. **The binding constraint is working capital, not demand.** Families either pay ~$78K up front and wait, or the school carries the receivable for 12–18 months. At a 12-student launch that is roughly **$940K of working capital**, and it is the single decision that determines whether this is a school for affluent families or a school with reach.
3. **The revenue stream is under active political attack from both flanks.** The Mamdani administration named private-school payments as a savings target against a **$5.4B budget gap**; FY26 budgeted **$935M against $1.3B actually spent in FY25**; and the equity critique now has hard numbers — **white students are 71% of tuition-payment recipients versus 12.5% of the public-school disability population** (Sources: [Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/05/06/nyc-special-education-carter-case-tuition-racial-disparities/), [Manhattan Institute](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED679657.pdf)).

**Our angle.** Prong two of the Carter test — *is the parent's placement appropriate?* — is won by testimony and documentation, not by accreditation. A long-tenured **Dean who has sat at CSE tables, written IEPs, and can testify credibly** is the exact asset that wins prong two, and a school designed from day one to produce a hearing-ready evidentiary record is a differentiated product in a market where, per the Manhattan Institute, incumbent schools **are not required to report outcomes at all**.

**Recommendation: Enter with conditions.** Site for NYC reach, capitalize the receivable, and build the outcome-reporting posture that survives the coming reform.

## 2. Market Definition

| Dimension | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Market** | Private day-school placements for K–12 students with disabilities, funded by district reimbursement or direct payment following unilateral parental placement (IDEA §1412(a)(10)(C); *Burlington*/*Carter*) |
| **Stage** | **Mature and politically contested** — not emerging. Twenty years of compounding growth ($47M in 2005 → $1.3B in FY25) with reform pressure now explicit |
| **Geography** | NYC five boroughs (primary demand); Nassau/Suffolk (secondary, thin); Westchester (adjacent) |
| **Buyer** | The parent chooses and enrolls; the district pays. Two customers, one product |
| **Tailwinds** | NYC disability classification rate rose from 139 to 242 per 1,000 students, a **74% increase** 2013-14 → 2024-25; NYC has *never* reached full IEP-service implementation (7% still partially or not served in 2023-24 = 13,003 students); OATH cut average case resolution from 282 days to **152 days in 2025**, shortening the receivable cycle; pendency locks in multi-year revenue |
| **Headwinds** | Explicit municipal reform pressure amid a $5.4B budget gap; racial-equity critique with hard numbers; Long Island's near-total absence from the filing data; families must front tuition or the school must; incumbent LI supply is **not constrained** (Winston Prep LI shows an 80% acceptance rate on rolling admission) |

See `docs/icp-002-carter-day-school.md` for the full profile. Summary:

| Segment | Who | Pain | Current solution | Trigger | WTP | Where | Decision maker |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| **P2 — Pendency continuer** | Family that already prevailed once; child in a placement that is closing, mismatched, or aging out of grade band | Needs a lateral placement that will not break pendency | Currently district-funded | Their school closes, or grade band ends | Same as P1, **but zero collection risk** | Attorney and school-transition networks | Parent + attorney |

It is also the segment that consumes working capital.

## 6. Pricing Analysis

### Market price points

| Reference | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Winston Prep LI | $50,000 | Grades 3–12, LI |
| Vincent Smith | **$78,500** | Grades 1–12, all related services included, 2026-27 |
| Stephen Gaynor | $86,220 | K–8, Manhattan, 2025-26 |
| **NYC average Carter settlement per student** | **$101,757** | May 2025; includes related services and fees; vs. $32,284 city per-pupil |
| NYC average, autism cases | ~$144,000 | Chalkbeat, 2026 |

### Pricing models in market

- **All-inclusive annual tuition** (Vincent Smith) — one number covering instruction and related services. Strategically superior for Carter: a single figure produces a cleaner hearing record and a simpler settlement, and avoids unbundled related-services line items the district can contest separately.
- **Tuition + separately billed related services** — more revenue at the margin, more litigation surface.
- **Prepay incentives** — Vincent Smith offers 10% off for payment by September 1. This is a working-capital instrument disguised as a discount.

### Recommendation

**$76,500 all-inclusive for grades 1–8, holding under both Vincent Smith and the average settlement.**

Rationale:
1. **Pricing below the average settlement is a sales feature, not a concession.** A $76,500 ask against a $101,757 average makes the district's settlement math easy and reduces the equitable-considerations attack surface.
2. **All-inclusive is the right structure** — it mirrors the strongest comp and produces the cleanest evidentiary record.
3. **Sitting just under Vincent Smith** positions on value without triggering a "cheap therefore inappropriate" argument at hearing. Do not price near Winston Prep's $50,000; in this market an unusually low price invites a challenge to program adequacy.
4. **Offer the 10% prepay discount** (it funds the receivable) **and simultaneously offer a $0-down direct-payment track** for P3 families. Two tracks, one price.

## 7. Unit Economics and the Working-Capital Question

**Assumptions:** classes of 6; NYS-certified SPED lead per class at $85K + 25% loaded; 0.5 shared TA per class; 1.0 related-services clinician per 12 students at $105K loaded; Dean/head $150K loaded; admin/ops $90K loaded; Nassau-or-outer-borough facility; insurance, curriculum, legal, tech $120K.

| | Year 1 (12 students) | Year 3 (42 students) | Year 5 (78 students) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross billings | $918,000 | $3,213,000 | $5,967,000 |
| Collection allowance | −15% | −12% | −10% |
| **Net revenue** | **$780,300** | **$2,827,440** | **$5,370,300** |
| Variable cost @ ~$31,000/student | $372,000 | $1,302,000 | $2,418,000 |
| Fixed (facility scaled) | $500,000 | $680,000 | $1,050,000 |
| **Total cost** | **$872,000** | **$1,982,000** | **$3,468,000** |
| **Surplus / (loss)** | **($91,700)** | **$845,440** | **$1,902,300** |
| Margin | (12%) | 30% | 35% |

**Breakeven: ~14 students.** That is a genuinely reachable Year-1 target and the headline good news of this analysis.

> ### The decision that actually matters
> **Working capital, not enrollment.** If families front tuition, you need almost no capital — and you have built Vincent Smith with a different logo, serving the same affluent segment, exposed to the same equity critique. If you carry the receivable so P3 families can attend, you need roughly **$940K of working capital at 12 students** and **$2.4M at 42**, recovered on a **~152-day average case-resolution cycle** (OATH, 2025) plus settlement-to-payment lag.
>
> Every strategic question in this report resolves to that one number. Options: a prepay-discount mix that self-funds a portion; a receivables facility priced against settled-case history; philanthropic first-loss capital; or a deliberate cap on the number of $0-down seats per cohort. **Model all four before siting the school.**

## 9. Moats and Defensibility

| Moat | Strength | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Pendency lock-in** | **Very strong** | Once a family prevails, stay-put obliges the district to keep funding the placement while any dispute is pending. Effectively multi-year contracted revenue per student with near-zero churn. The best retention mechanic available in this sector |
| **Attorney-network trust** | **Strong, slow** | Concentrated, relationship-based, and hard to buy. Two to three years to build; durable once built |
| **Founder credibility as hearing witness** | **Strong, unusual** | A Dean with district-side CSE tenure is the witness who wins prong two. Most competing schools cannot put someone with that résumé on the stand |
| **Published outcome data** | **Available, unclaimed** | The Manhattan Institute's sharpest critique is that these schools report *nothing* — no progress, graduation, attendance, or postsecondary data. Being the first to publish is both a marketing asset and reform insurance |
| **Regulatory barrier** | **None — by design** | Door D's advantage is no approval requirement. It is also why anyone else can enter |
| **Switching costs (family)** | **High** | Mid-stream transfer risks breaking pendency; families do not move once funded |
| **Brand** | **Weak at launch** | Vincent Smith has 102 years. This is a real handicap in a market where prong two rewards track record |

## 12. Go / No-Go Signal

### **Enter with conditions.**

**The model works.** Breakeven at ~14 students, 30% margins at 42, pendency-locked retention, no regulatory approval gate, no NYSED rate cap, no 16-student floor, no 35% overhead parameter, and a founder whose credentials are the exact asset the legal test rewards. Against Door A — where the same founder would face a regional-need survey designed to screen out this student profile, a state-set tuition rate, a two-year cash lag, and a ban on any parent payment — Door D is the materially better vehicle. That conclusion is firm.

**Three conditions, all binding:**

1. **Resolve geography before anything else.** The Carter market is 92–98% New York City. Either site the school where Brooklyn and Manhattan families can reach it, or commit explicitly to being a Nassau school that recruits Queens and NYC families with transportation — and validate assumption #3 before signing a lease. A Nassau campus serving Nassau families is a $20–30M pond in which two established schools already have unfilled seats.
2. **Capitalize the receivable before enrolling a single $0-down family.** Roughly $940K at 12 students. Decide the instrument — prepay mix, receivables facility, philanthropic first-loss, or a hard cap on carried seats — before opening.
3. **Build for the reform, not around it.** Price under the settlement average, publish outcome data no incumbent publishes, and serve the segment the equity critique says is excluded. When caps arrive, the schools that survive will be the ones that are demonstrably cheaper, demonstrably effective, and demonstrably not serving only affluent families.

### Next steps (next 90 days)

1. Call 10 of the 16 special-education firms Vincent Smith publicly lists. Ask two questions: *would you refer to a first-year school led by a career Dean*, and *what is your actual filing-to-payment timeline*. This single exercise validates assumptions #3, #5, and #6.
2. FOIL NYC DOE for Carter placements by grade band and disability category (assumptions #1, #2).
3. Interview 15 families from the existing summer-program base on the $0-down track (assumption #4).
4. Model the four working-capital instruments side by side.
5. Retain NY special-education counsel — for the enrollment contract, the direct-payment structure, and the SRO tuition-reasonableness review.
6. **Keep running the summer intensive.** It is the pipeline, the outcome dataset, and the proof of practice that a first-year school otherwise lacks.
